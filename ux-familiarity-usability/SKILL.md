# Familiarity & Perceived Usability

Help users understand how an interface works through appropriate conventions and a coherent visual presentation.

## Working approach

Identify the audience, platform, primary task, and existing design system. Infer likely conventions from the product and available evidence; do not assume every audience uses the same products. Inspect both appearance and behavior when possible. A screenshot alone cannot establish interaction usability.

For a review, distinguish unfamiliar behavior from visual inconsistency. For requested implementation, make targeted changes that preserve the product's identity and working patterns.

## Apply the two laws

- **Jakob's Law:** Users carry expectations from other interfaces. Prefer established conventions for navigation, links, buttons, form behavior, selection, saving, and feedback when they fit the audience and task. Make repeated controls behave consistently within the product. Use familiar language and communicate state clearly. A deliberate departure can be appropriate when it solves a demonstrated problem; make its behavior discoverable and verify it. Do not copy another product's appearance or workflow without considering context.
- **Aesthetic Usability Effect:** Coherent typography, spacing, alignment, and visual treatment can improve perceived ease of use. Improve those qualities within the existing design system. Keep perceived usability separate from observed task success: an attractive screen may still have confusing labels, broken interactions, poor contrast, or hidden errors. Do not treat visual polish as evidence that the task works.

Neither law has a standard numerical formula. Treat shorthand such as “familiarity reduces relearning” and “appeal can improve perceived usability” as tendencies, not calculable guarantees.

## Verify

Walk through a representative task using the expected control behavior. Check navigation, primary actions, system status, recovery, and terminology wherever affected. Inspect relevant loading, empty, error, focus, and disabled states for visual consistency and legibility. When user research is available, distinguish aesthetic ratings from completion, error, and comprehension measures. Without that evidence, label claims about user perception as hypotheses.

## Report

For each material issue, identify the location, expected convention or visual cue, observed mismatch, and proposed or completed change. Explain the user's likely confusion in concrete terms. Report functional and visual verification separately. Retain deliberate, effective conventions rather than forcing a generic redesign or treating novelty itself as a defect.

## Reference

[Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/) describes Jakob's Law and the Aesthetic Usability Effect. The working rules above apply those principles without treating familiarity or beauty as a substitute for task evidence.
